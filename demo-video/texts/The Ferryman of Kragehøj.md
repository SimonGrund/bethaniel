# The Ferryman of Kragehøj

The ferry left Kragehøj at six, as it had every morning since Mr Hale's father built the landing. From the water, Kragehøj looked smaller than it was: a church, a smokehouse, and a row of tarred cottages leaning into the wind like old men at a bar.

Mr Hale kept a copy of *The Book of Tides* in the wheelhouse. He had never read past the first chapter, but he liked to hold it when the fog came down, the way some men hold a cigarette they do not light.

"Three miles to the Hollow," he said to the girl in the yellow coat. "Less if the glimmerwick is running."

She did not know what glimmerwick was. Nobody from the mainland did. It was the pale current that came up from the deep in late autumn, and the old people of Kragehøj said it carried the voices of the drowned. The young people said it carried mackerel. Mr Hale said nothing at all, which was his way of agreeing with both.

The Hollow was not really a place. It was a gap in the reef where the sea went quiet, and every ferryman on that coast slowed down there without being told to. Mr Hale's father had slowed down there. His grandfather had slowed down there. When the glimmerwick was running, even the gulls went silent.

"Why do they call it the Hollow?" the girl asked.

"Because that is what it is," said Mr Hale.

She wrote something in a notebook. She had been writing in it since Kragehøj, small careful lines, and twice she had looked up at him as though checking a detail. He did not ask. Forty years on the ferry had taught him that people who came to Kragehøj in October came for a reason, and the reason was nearly always a grave.

At the Hollow the engine dropped to a murmur. The water turned the colour of pewter, and under it, faint as breath on glass, the glimmerwick moved.

"My grandmother was born in Kragehøj," the girl said. "She left when she was nine. She never said why."

Mr Hale looked at the water for a long time. Then he reached for *The Book of Tides*, opened it to the first chapter, and handed it to her. On the flyleaf, in faded brown ink, was a name he had not said aloud in forty years.

"She said why," he said. "She just did not say it to you."

Two miles further on, the mainland came up out of the fog, grey and ordinary, and the girl in the yellow coat closed the book and held it the way he did, without reading.
