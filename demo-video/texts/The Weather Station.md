# The Weather Station

## One

The road to the weather station climbed for eleven kilometers through spruce forest and then broke sudenly onto open ground, and it was there, at the treeline, that Marta always stoped the truck and got out to look at the valley. She had made the drive four hundred times, posibly more, and the habit had survived every attempt to abandon it. Her predecesor had warned her about this. He had told her that the mountain would install routines in her without asking, and that resisting them was more exhausting than submitting.

The valley in October was the color of wet cardboard. Somewhere below, obscured by a persistant bank of low cloud, the village continued its busines without her, and would continue it for the fourten days she was scheduled to spend at the sumit. She counted the transmission towers on the opposite ridge, which was another routine, and found the usual seven, and got back into the truck.

The station itself was an unremarkable building, a two-story concrete box painted a shade of institutional yelow that had faded unevenly on the southern wall. It had been built in nineten sixty-eight by an agency that no longer existed, transfered twice between departments, and forgoten with sufficient thoroughness that its budget had never been seriously reveiwed. Marta considered this the single most importent fact about her employer. Obscurity was a kind of funding.

Inside, the equipment humed. The anemometer log had recorded a gust of ninety-four kilometers per hour at three in the morning, which was unremarkable for the season but would need to be entered manualy because the automatic uplink had failed again. She made coffee, opened the maintenence ledger, and began the slow work of reconciling what the instrumants had measured against what they had managed to report.

The discrepancy was eleven hours. Somewhere between midnight and eleven in the morning the station had gone quiet, and the cantral office had not noticed, and nobody had telephoned. Marta wrote the interval in the ledger and then, after a momant, added a note in the margin: *nobody called.* She had been keping a private record of these silences for two years. It was not evidance of anything in particular. It was simply a thing she had decided to know.

## Two

Her supervisor was a courteous man named Halvorsen who worked from an ofice three hundred kilometers away and had visated the station exactly once, during his first month in the post, seven years earlier. He communicated by email, in peragraphs of considerable formality, and had a habit of thanking her for observations he had clearly not read.

She composed the outage report carefuly. She had learned that a report which merly stated a problem would be acknowledged and filed, whereas a report which quantified the problem in monetary terms would ocasionally produce a response. So she calculated the cost of the lost data — a figure she constructed from the station's anual operating budget divided by the hours in a year, multipleid by eleven — and put it in the first sentence.

The reply arrived four days later and thanked her for her observations.

Marta printed it and pinned it to the corkboard beside the window, where it joined a considerable archive of similar documants. The corkboard was, she thought, the most honest instrumant in the building. It measured something real and it had never once failed to record it.

Outside, the weather did what it had been doing for the eleven days since her arival, which was very little. The barometric presure held steady. The wind came from the northwest at betwen twelve and twenty kilometers per hour. The temperature descended gradualy toward the first hard frost of the year, which the models suggested would arrive on the sixteanth and which, in Marta's private and unrecorded opinion, would arive on the fourteenth.

She had been right about the first frost for six consecutive years. She had never mantioned this to anybody, partly because it would have been dificult to explain and partly because she suspected the explanation was unremarkible: she had simply been in the same place long enough that the place had begun to inform her. There was no mysticism in it. There was only duration.

## Three

On the twelfth day a hiker appeared on the access road.

This was sufficiently unusual that Marta watched him for several minutes through the observation window before going down to meet him. He was young, perhaps twanty-five, and dressed with the particular over-preparednes of somebody who had bought everything at once from a single shop. His pack was enormous. He was walking badly.

"Is this the meteorological station?" he asked, when he was close enough to be heard.

"It is."

"I'm sorry. I know it isn't open to the public."

"There is no public," Marta said. "There's a road and a building. You've found both."

His name was Erik and he was writing a dissertation on infrastructure abandonmant, which he explained at some length while she made him cofee and examined his left ankle, which had swollen impressivly above the boot. He had come to look at the station because it appeared on a list of faciliteis scheduled for decommissioning in the folowing budgetary cycle.

Marta put down the coffee pot.

"Which list?"

He showed her, on his telephone, a document she had never seen. It was a departmantal planning annex, three years old, and it listed the station under a heading that translated approximately as *asets pending consolidation.* Her own name did not apear. The station's designation had been abreviated in a way that she recognized only because she had spent two decades reading exactly this kind of abbreviation.

"They didn't tell you," Erik said. It was not quite a question.

"They may have thought they had."

She read the annex twice. The relevant peragraph committed the department to nothing whatsoever. It observed that certain facilities had been idantified. It noted that consolidation would be considered. It contained four seperate constructions in the conditional mood, and Marta, who had read a great many such documents, understod that this was neither a decision nor an accidant. It was a placeholder for a decision somebody intended to make later, written in language that would embarass nobody if the decision went the other way.

"It's not a closure notice," she said.

"No."

"It's a way of not having to write one."

## Four

Erik stayed two nights, because his ankle required it and because the alternative was walking eleven kilometers downhil on a joint that would not tolerate it. He was excellant company in the specific sense that he asked questions and then listened to the answers, which Marta had come to regard as an uncomon accomplishment.

She showed him the instruments. The anemometer, which was original to the building and had been repaired eleven times. The precipitation gauge, replaced in two thousand and four. The thermometer housing, which she had rebuilt herself one winter using materials ordered under a maintenance code intended for something else entirely, a small administrative fraud she described without embarrassment.

"Nobody checks," she said. "That's the whole of it. Nobody has checked anything here since Halvorsen's predecessor retired, and she retired in two thousand and eleven."

"Doesn't that bother you?"

Marta considered the question seriously, because it deserved to be considered seriously and because she had asked it of herself many times without arriving anywhere.

"It bothers me on Tuesdays," she said evantually. "Most of the rest of the time I think it's the reason the station still exists. Something that nobody examines is also something that nobody cancels. The examination and the cancelation arrive together, in the same envelope. So I have spent twenty years being extremly careful not to be interesting."

Erik wrote this down, which she had not anticipated and did not object to.

"And now I've made you interesting," he said.

"You've found a documant. The document was already there."

## Five

The frost arrived on the fourteanth, as she had known it would.

She went out at half past five with a torch and walked the perimeter, checking the instrument enclosures for the particular kind of icing that would corrupt a reading without trigering an alarm. The grass crunched. Her breath hung. The valley below was invisable under a lid of cloud that would not lift until miday, and the sky above was extraordinarily clear, and for perhaps ten minutes she stood at the edge of the compound and loked at it and thought about nothing at all.

It occurred to her afterwards, walking back, that she had spent a considerible proportion of her adult life in circumstances that would strike most people as intolerible, and had been, by any measure she could construct, contanted. She was not certain what to do with this observation. It did not amount to an argument for anything. It was simply true, and she had noticed it, and noticing was most of what she did.

At six she entered the frost in the log. At seven she sent the daily report. At eight she began drafting an email to Halvorsen which she rewrote nine times over the following two days and did not send.
