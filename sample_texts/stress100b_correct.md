# The Night Ferry

The ferry to Skarholm ran twice a day in summer and once in winter, and Ines had taken the winter crossing so often that she no longer bothered to watch the harbor slide away behind her. She sat in the same seat each time, portside, three rows from the stern, where the engine noise was loud enough to discourage conversation but not so loud that she could not read.

She had been the island's only doctor for eleven years. Before that there had been a man named Ostergaard who had stayed for thirty and left without ceremony, and before him nobody could remember clearly. The islanders spoke of Ostergaard the way people speak of weather that has already passed: with a kind of settled grievance that had lost its heat.

The letter in her coat pocket had come from the mainland hospital, and she had read it four times without deciding what to do about it. They wanted her to take a post in the cardiology unit, starting in September. The salary was nearly double what the island paid. The letter was polite, brief, and entirely reasonable, and she resented it for that.

Outside, the water had gone the color of pewter. A gull kept pace with the ferry for a while, hanging in the air beside the window with an expression of enormous patience, and then it peeled away toward the shipping lane and was gone.

Ines thought about Mrs Lund, who was eighty-four and refused to be moved to the mainland, and who would almost certainly die within the year of something that a mainland hospital could have managed easily. She thought about the Berg boy, whose asthma she had learned to hear before he did. She thought about the fact that neither of these was an argument, exactly, and that both of them felt like one.

The crossing took ninety minutes. She spent most of it not reading.

Skarholm appeared the way it always did, low and gray and longer than it looked on a map, with the church tower at the western end and the fish sheds crowding the harbor. The ferry swung wide to clear the sandbank and came in against the pier with a shudder that Ines felt through the soles of her boots.

Petra was waiting on the quay, which was unusual. Petra ran the island's only shop and did not, as a rule, meet boats.

"There's been a fall," she said, before Ines had finished stepping off the ramp. "Mrs Lund. She's at home. Sigrid is with her."

"When?"

"An hour ago. Maybe two. She wouldn't let anyone call you on the ferry."

Of course she wouldn't, Ines thought, and did not say. She shifted her bag to her other shoulder and started up the hill without waiting to see whether Petra would follow.

The Lund house stood at the top of the village, where the road gave up pretending to be a road and became a track. It had been painted yellow once. Sigrid met her at the door with the particular composure of someone who has decided in advance not to be frightened.

"She's talking," Sigrid said. "She's cross. That seems like a good sign to me, but I don't know."

Mrs Lund was on the floor of the front room with a cushion under her head and a blanket over her, and she was, as advertised, cross.

"I told her not to bother you," she said. "You've been on the boat."

"I've been on the boat a thousand times." Ines knelt, put her bag down, and began the slow ordinary business of finding out what had happened. Pulse. Pupils. The hip, which was the thing she was most afraid of. Mrs Lund submitted to all of it with an air of being interrupted.

"It's not broken," Ines said eventually.

"I know it isn't."

"You don't know that. You couldn't have known that."

"I know what broken feels like," Mrs Lund said. "I broke my wrist in nineteen sixty-one and I have not forgotten it."

Ines sat back on her heels. The relief arrived late, the way it always did, several seconds behind the certainty. "You've bruised the hip badly and you've frightened Sigrid, and you're going to let me look at you again tomorrow."

"I'll be at the shop tomorrow."

"You'll be in that chair tomorrow."

They regarded each other. Somewhere behind them Sigrid let out a small breath that might have been a laugh.

Later, when Mrs Lund had been moved to the chair and fed and left in the care of a neighbor, Ines walked back down through the village in the dark. The wind had turned. She could hear the ferry's engines starting up at the pier for the return crossing, and she stood for a moment at the turn of the road and listened to them.

The letter was still in her pocket. It would still be there in the morning, and in September, and the hospital would find somebody else eventually, and none of that had anything to do with Mrs Lund's hip.

That was the trouble with the island. Nothing that happened here ever amounted to an argument. It only ever amounted to a Tuesday, and then another one.

She went home and put the kettle on and did not open the letter again.

The next morning was clear and very cold, and the island looked, as it sometimes did after a hard night, like a photograph of itself.

Ines walked up to the Lund house before surgery and found Mrs Lund in the chair, exactly where she had been left, with an expression of tremendous virtue.

"I have not moved," she announced.

"Sigrid told me you tried to."

"Trying is not moving."

The hip had come up purple along the whole length of the thigh, which was ugly and entirely expected. Ines pressed along the bone with two fingers, watching the old woman's face rather than her own hands, and found nothing that made either of them flinch.

"You were lucky," she said.

"I was careful."

"You were on a step stool at eighty-four."

"I was reaching the good plates," Mrs Lund said, as though this settled it, and in a sense it did.

Surgery ran from nine until one, in the front two rooms of the house that the parish had given the island's doctor sometime before the war. There were eleven patients that morning, which was a great many for Skarholm in February. Two of them had nothing wrong with them at all and had come because they had heard about Mrs Lund and wanted to be told about it by someone who knew.

Ines told them nothing, at some length, and they went away satisfied.

The Berg boy came at half past eleven with his mother, who was worried, and with a chest that sounded very slightly worse than it had in December. Ines listened for a long time, moving the bell of the stethoscope across his back while he breathed obediently in and out.

"He's been running," his mother said. "In the cold. I've told him."

"Running is good for him."

"Not in the cold."

"Especially in the cold, if we get the dose right." Ines wrote out a new prescription and handed it over, and then, because the boy was watching her with the flat unimpressed stare of an eleven-year-old who has been discussed in the third person for ten minutes, she added, "You'll be able to run further by spring. That's the point of it."

He considered this. "How much further?"

"That depends how much you run."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Ines agreed. "It isn't."

After surgery she ate standing at the window and watched the light change over the water. The mainland was not visible from Skarholm, not even on the clearest day. That was the first thing everyone said about the island and the only thing that was reliably true about it.

She thought about the cardiology unit, briefly and without much feeling, the way you think about a room you once looked at and did not rent. Then she washed the plate and went out to see the Nielsens' new baby, who was four days old and, according to the health visitor's note, feeding badly.

The baby was feeding fine. The mother was not sleeping, which was a different problem and a harder one, and Ines sat in the kitchen for an hour longer than the visit required and drank a cup of coffee she did not want.

By the time she got home the light had gone entirely. She lit the stove, and stood in front of it with her coat still on, and thought about how she would write the reply. Something short. Something that did not sound like a refusal of the mainland so much as an acceptance of the island, which was not the same thing and which she had never once managed to explain to anybody who had asked.

She had not written it by the time she went to bed. She had not written it by the end of the week either, and by then the ferry had come and gone four more times, and the letter had softened at the corners from being carried about, and Mrs Lund was back at the shop against all instruction, telling everybody who came in that she had never been in the slightest danger.
