The Lighthouse Keeper's Letter

Mara went up the spiral staircase for the very first time in twenty long years. The iron steps made a groaning sound beneath her boots, and the salty salt air pressed against the narrow windows in a way that was like an old, insistent friend. Her grandfather, who was her grandfather, had kept this lighthouse for a period of four decades, and now, three months after his funeral, the deed had finally at last passed to her.

At the top, the lantern room was exactly as she remembered it to be: brass fittings polished to a soft glow, the great lens silent and watchful, a logbook that was open on the small oak desk. She set down her bag and ran a finger along the windowsill. There was no dust there. Someone had been there in that place recently.

A folded sheet of paper lay tucked beneath the logbook, with her name written on the front of it in her grandfather's careful handwriting hand. Mara sat herself down before she opened it up, because her knees had begun the process of trembling and she did not really want to admit to herself why this was happening.

"Mara," the letter began to say, "if you are reading this letter, then I have finally run out of excuses to tell you the truth by myself in person. Please forgive an old man for his cowardice."

She read slowly and carefully. He wrote about the night her mother had left, and he wrote about the storm that had driven a small fishing boat onto the rocks below the cliff, and he wrote about the woman that he had pulled from the surf and carried up these very stairs. He wrote about the choice that he had made to keep her secret, and he wrote about the daughter that that woman had left behind in his care to look after.

"You were three years old at the time," he wrote in his writing. "You called me Grandfather because that is what I asked you to call me. I am sorry about it. I loved you no less for the lie that I told, and perhaps a little bit more, because I knew what it cost me to do."

Mara set the letter down on the desk. Outside, gulls were turning slow circles above the water, and the sea was moving with the same patient rhythm that it had kept since long before the time of her birth. She thought to herself that she really ought to feel angry, or betrayed, or unmoored in some way. Instead, she felt only a quiet, surprising tenderness, almost as though a door that she had never noticed before had opened up in a familiar room that she knew well.

She closed the book in a closing motion. She stood up, walked over to the great lens, and laid her palm flat against the cool glass of it. The sea stretched away in every single direction, and somewhere out there, she now understood at this point, was a story that belonged to her and that she had only just barely begun to learn about.

She would stay the night there. In the morning, she would light the lamp up.
