# The Ferryman's Ledger

The ferry stopped running in October, and by November the river had frozen hard enough to walk on. Ilse Rasmussen had lived on the far bank for thirty-one years, and in all that time she had never once crossed the ice.

Her father had drowned under it when she was nine. He had been the ferryman then, as his mother had been before him, and the ledger he kept — every crossing, every fare, every name — sat on the shelf above the stove where he had left it. Ilse had not opened it since the funeral.

The letter came on a Thursday, carried by a boy who had walked the long way round by the bridge. It was from the county, and it said the ferry licence would lapse at the end of the year unless someone claimed it. There was a form to sign. There was a box to tick.

She read it twice, put it on the table, and looked at the ledger.

That evening she took it down. The spine cracked when she opened it, and a pressed leaf fell out from between two pages, brown and brittle as paper. Her father's handwriting ran down the columns in a small, careful hand: the date, the passenger, the fare, the weather. Nothing else. No comment, no complaint, no mention of the cold.

She turned to the last page he had written. The final entry was dated the day before he died, and it recorded a single crossing, made at dusk, with no fare taken. In the passenger column he had written one word: *Ilse.*

She had no memory of it. She had been nine years old, and she had crossed the river with her father on the last evening of his life, and she did not remember a moment of it.

The next morning she walked down to the landing. The ice was grey and unmarked, and the far bank looked closer than it had ever looked from the boat. She stood there until her hands ached with the cold, and then she went back up to the house and signed the form.

She has kept the ledger since. There is a new column in it now, in her own hand, and it records the same four things her father recorded and nothing more. People who cross tell her she should write down what they say, the strange things and the funny ones, and she tells them she is not that kind of ferryman. Then she smiles, because it is not entirely true, and she carries the stories home in her head instead.
